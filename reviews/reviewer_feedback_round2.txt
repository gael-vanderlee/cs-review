Reviewer Comments


Reviewer: 1

Recommendation: Author Should Prepare A Minor Revision

Comments:
Title, abstract, and keywords. The title is appropriate and faithfully reflects the content, and it is a clear improvement over the previous version. The abstract represents the paper accurately. The keywords work, but I would suggest adding EEG and cybersickness so the paper is discoverable by researchers searching those exact terms.
Introduction. The introduction is a strong improvement over the previous version and clarifies the points raised in the earlier review cycle. My one suggestion is that the authors' own contributions are easy to miss. I would encourage them to highlight these more explicitly. In the Introduction section, I suggest the authors either add a dedicated "Contributions" subsection by emphasising the relevant text, or use another visual aid. The material is strong, and signposting it would ensure readers do not overlook it.
Organization, focus, and length. The manuscript is well organised, and the length is appropriate for the topic. The reformatted table is a very useful addition and the new figures are helpful. The paper is focused, which makes the main discussion points easy to follow. One suggestion: in the future directions section, the authors do a strong job of laying out the directions, but each is presented as a single paragraph beginning "First," "Second," and so on. Breaking these into sub-subsections or otherwise highlighting them would make this valuable material easier to navigate and harder to overlook.
Readability. Readability is excellent. EEG papers can easily become dense, heavy, and hard to read, and the authors have done a very good job of keeping the content accessible while preserving its complexity. The pacing is strong and the manuscript reads easily from start to finish.
Novelty, impact, and replicability. The paper is novel, and the authors clearly differentiate their work from Chang et al. Beyond surveying the neural correlates of cybersickness, its strongest contribution is critical: it surfaces inconsistencies in how specific EEG bands relate to cybersickness, and it points to broader flaws across cybersickness research, including the unclear threshold between sick and non-sick states and an over-reliance on subjective markers that makes a robust objective marker hard to establish. It also calls out the lack of methodological transparency that commonly affects EEG and other neurophysiological cybersickness studies, and it questions the vague use of "neuromarker" language. These are valuable contributions, and the community and beyond would benefit from reading this paper. On replicability, the method is reproducible: the authors clarified their methodology across the revision so that the survey itself can be reproduced, which is fitting for a paper whose central critique is underreporting.
